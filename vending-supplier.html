A vending supplier typically falls into one of three buckets: manufacturer-direct, distributor/wholesaler, or importer/private-label consolidator. Each has a predictable set of tradeoffs in MOQ, case quantities, price tiers, and risk. **Manufacturer-direct** means you buy from the brand owner or the factory that produces the item. This can work for high-volume staples (snacks, beverages, consumables), especially if you can hit pallet quantities. The upside is pricing and fewer middlemen. The downside is MOQs, less flexibility on mixed-SKU orders, and sometimes slower handling for small accounts. **Distributor/wholesaler** is the common path for route operators because it matches vending reality: mixed cases, frequent reorders, and a wide catalog. You pay margin for that convenience. The real discriminator is not the catalog size, it’s whether they ship what you ordered, when you needed it, with the dating you require. **Importer/consolidator (including “merchant supplier” models)** buys from upstream factories and resells domestically. This is where you can find unique SKUs or better unit economics, but you inherit more compliance and continuity risk, and you need to understand shipping terms and lead times. If your discovery source is a **vendor marketplace**, treat it as lead generation, not validation. Marketplaces are good at showing options, bad at proving operational reliability. It tells you what “registered facility” actually means in practice. ## How do you compare pricing, case packs, and reorder reliability? ![Two different case packs and a calculator illustrating landed cost comparison by unit – pricing comparison](https://fwxdribwlccxnmrdzsfs.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/article-images/8470d2cec5f19b17/ai/two-different-case-packs-and.webp) Pricing comparison fails in vending when you compare the wrong unit. You need **landed cost per sellable unit** at your real reorder quantities, with case pack and tier breaks accounted for, and then you need to stress-test that against reorder reliability. ### Step 1: Normalize to landed cost per sellable unit A vending supplier quote that looks cheaper often hides one of three things: a larger case pack than you can move before dating risk, freight that flips the math, or tiered pricing that only applies at unrealistic quantities. Use this structure: - Unit cost at your quantity tier (not the “starting at” price) - Case pack size (units per case) and whether mixed cases are allowed - Freight and surcharges to your dock (or to each route if you break down shipments) - Damage/shortage assumptions based on their policy and your experience A simple comparison table forces honesty: | Supplier | SKU | Price tier basis | Case pack | Freight terms | Landed cost per unit | Notes | | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | | A | Item X | 1-10 cases | 24 | Delivered | $X.XX | No substitutions | | B | Item X | 1-5 cases | 30 | Freight added | $X.XX | Short dating risk | | C | Item X | 1 case | 24 | Pickup | $X.XX | Requires will-call | If you are evaluating many sources from a marketplace, you need deduplication. The same supplier can appear across dozens of listings, which makes “comparison” feel like progress while you’re really just sorting duplicates. BuyerPilot’s [automatic supplier deduplication for repeated sellers](https://buyerpilot.github.io/supplier-deduplication.html) exists specifically to prevent that trap. ### Step 2: Compare MOQ vs case quantities like an operator, not a buyer MOQ is common in factory sourcing; case quantities are the day-to-day reality in vending replenishment. The key is matching order size to velocity and shelf-life. A low unit price at high MOQ is useless if the product expires before it sells, or if it ties up cash that should be in higher-turn SKUs. For each candidate supplier, document: - Minimum order rules (by dollars, cases, pallets, or weight) - Mixed-case allowances and pick fees - Short-dated discount programs (and whether you can refuse them) ### Step 3: Validate reorder reliability with two policies and one test order Reorder reliability is mostly explained by (1) how they handle out-of-stocks and (2) how they communicate. Ask for their written policy on backorders and substitutions. If they cannot provide it, assume the worst. Then run a small but realistic test order. Include one fast mover and one long-tail SKU. You’re testing the system: fill rate, packing accuracy, dating, and how they behave when something is unavailable. If you want to formalize this like procurement teams do, look at the structure of a supplier performance metrics scorecard. The ISO 9001 standard is often referenced for supplier control in quality management systems; ISO publishes the standard at [ISO 9001 quality management](https://www.iso.org/iso-9001-quality-management.html), and while you don’t need certification to run a vending route, the discipline of documented criteria and corrective actions translates well. ## What due diligence reduces stockout and compliance risk? Due diligence for a vending supplier is less about corporate paperwork and more about preventing three operational failures: machines empty, product not saleable, and product you should not have stocked in the first place. ### Stockout risk: lead times, fill rate, and substitutions Get the basics in writing: cutoff times, ship days, carrier options, and whether they ship partials. If they ship partials, confirm whether they split freight charges or you pay twice. If they substitute, require explicit approval for any SKU change. “Equivalent item” is a phrase that causes route-level chaos. Also ask how they handle allocations during shortages. A supplier that allocates fairly to existing accounts can be worth more than a cheaper source that ships to whoever yells loudest that week. ### Compliance and restricted products: know what you are allowed to sell and source Compliance varies by country and product category, but the high-risk areas in vending are consistent: age-restricted products, regulated supplements, products with specific labeling requirements, and anything with temperature control requirements. For US operators, the FDA’s labeling rules are a reference point even when you are not the manufacturer. The FDA’s page on [food labeling and nutrition](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-labeling-nutrition) is a practical starting place to understand what “properly labeled” means. If a supplier is importing or relabeling, you need to know who is responsible for compliance, and you need traceability (lot codes, invoices, and recall contact). Expiration dating is where compliance meets profitability. Set a minimum remaining shelf-life requirement on receipt, and enforce it. If a supplier pushes short-dated inventory to hit their own turns, your machines become their clearance channel. ### Shipping terms, damage, and returns: control the hidden costs Shipping terms decide who owns the problem when a case arrives crushed or short. For importers, Incoterms matter. The International Chamber of Commerce maintains the official Incoterms framework; their overview at [ICC Incoterms rules](https://iccwbo.org/business-solutions/incoterms-rules/) is the authoritative reference for what terms like FOB and DDP actually mean. Returns policy should be evaluated like a contract, even if it’s a web page. Confirm: claims window, whether you need to keep packaging, whether they issue call tags, and how credits are applied. A generous return policy that requires a four-week approval cycle still hurts cash flow. ## Build a documented shortlist with a comparison tool mindset A shortlist is defensible when someone else can look at your notes and reach the same conclusion. Your leverage comes from being easy to serve and predictable, not from negotiating like a big-box retailer. ## Frequently Asked Questions **What are the 5 key supplier evaluation criteria?** For vending, the five that matter most are landed cost, fill rate, lead time consistency, substitution behavior, and expiration dating discipline. If you score only price and ignore reliability, you will pay for it in stockouts and waste. **What are the four types of suppliers?** A practical breakdown is manufacturer-direct, distributor/wholesaler, importer/consolidator, and local cash-and-carry or club-style retail supply. The right mix depends on your SKU velocity, storage capacity, and compliance risk tolerance. **What is another word for deduplication?** Common alternatives are de-duplication, duplicate removal, or record matching. In sourcing, it often means identifying when multiple listings or catalog entries point to the same underlying supplier. **What are Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 suppliers?** Tiering describes distance from the end brand: Tier 1 supplies the brand or retailer directly, Tier 2 supplies Tier 1, and Tier 3 is further upstream (raw materials or components). For vending inventory, you usually interact with Tier 1 distributors or importers, unless you buy manufacturer-direct.
