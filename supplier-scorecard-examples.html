The fastest way to break a scorecard is to score everything. You end up “averaging” a supplier that should have been eliminated. Use this rule: **anything that creates an irreversible business risk becomes a gate**. Everything else becomes a weighted score. Gates that usually belong in pass-fail for Alibaba and 1688 sourcing: - **MOQ and packaging constraints** that force unworkable inventory or carton quantities - **Sample availability and sample lead time** when you cannot validate without a physical sample - **Verification basics** (company identity, obvious red flags, inconsistent company info) - **Trade terms you require** (Trade Assurance on Alibaba if your process mandates it, specific payment terms, or shipping capability) - **Compliance evidence** for regulated categories Signals that usually work better as scored metrics: Price tiers by quantity, lead time, response speed, years active, ratings and reviews, repurchase or reorder indicators (common on 1688), and business type hints (factory vs trading company). These are comparative. A supplier can be “good enough” on one and exceptional on another, and the weighting should decide. This is also where deduplication matters. If the same supplier appears under five listings, your scorecard gets polluted with duplicates and you waste time “scoring” the same entity repeatedly. A dedicated step for [automatic supplier deduplication across listings](https://buyerpilot.github.io/supplier-deduplication.html) keeps your scorecard aligned to suppliers, not products. ## How do you set weights for price tiers, MOQ, and verification? ![A balance scale beside tier pricing and MOQ notes, showing how to weight cost and risk factors – weighting model](https://fwxdribwlccxnmrdzsfs.supabase.co/storage/v1/object/public/article-images/8470d2cec5f19b17/ai/a-balance-scale-beside-tier.webp) Weights should reflect what actually moves your landed cost and your failure risk at your planned order quantity. That sounds obvious, but most buyers accidentally weight the cheapest visible price tier, which is often irrelevant. Start by defining your scoring scale (I prefer 0-10 per metric because it is easy to normalize), then allocate weight across a small set of drivers. Keep it stable across a project so comparisons remain meaningful. ### A weighting template you can copy (general consumer goods) | Metric | Weight | Notes for Alibaba/1688 | | --- | ---: | --- | | Tiered unit price at target quantity | 35% | Score against your quantity tier; include tooling or setup fees separately | | MOQ fit | 15% | Penalize MOQs that force overbuy; do not “average it out” | | Lead time (sample + production) | 20% | Split into sample lead time and production lead time if needed | | Verification and marketplace trust | 15% | Verified supplier status, years active, Trade Assurance where relevant | | Responsiveness and clarity | 15% | Response speed plus specificity, not friendliness | Two practical details make this work in real life: First, treat **MOQ** as both a gate and a score depending on your situation. If anything above 2,000 units is impossible, set MOQ as a gate. If you can flex between 500 and 2,000, gate only the extremes and score the rest. Second, break “price” into the price you will actually pay. For a price comparison spreadsheet, the cleanest approach is to calculate an evaluated unit price at your target quantity and currency, then score suppliers relative to the best value. If you are building this in Excel, Microsoft’s documentation for [XLOOKUP](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/xlookup-function-b7fd680e-6d10-43e6-84f9-88eae8bf5929) is the most reliable way to pull the correct tier price from a tier table without fragile nested IFs. ### How to score tiered pricing without getting tricked On Alibaba and 1688, the “headline” price is frequently the lowest tier, sometimes tied to unrealistic quantities. Your scorecard should store: - Target quantity (the quantity you are evaluating) - The tier bracket that matches that quantity - Unit price for that bracket - Notes on what is included (packaging, printing, accessories) When you do this consistently, the price score becomes meaningful and you can defend it internally. It also makes supplier negotiation cleaner because you are comparing equivalent tiers. ### Where verification belongs (and where it does not) Verification signals reduce certain types of risk, but they do not guarantee quality. Use them as a meaningful weight, then force proof through samples, QC plans, and clear specs. For Alibaba-specific protection, Alibaba publishes the scope of its buyer protection programs and how disputes work. Keep your team aligned on what Trade Assurance covers by referencing [Alibaba Trade Assurance overview](https://www.alibaba.com/trade-assurance) during scorecard design, not after a problem happens. ## How do you capture evidence so the scorecard is auditable? An auditable scorecard is one where a teammate can answer, “Why did we rank Supplier B above Supplier A?” without reopening 30 tabs and guessing. The trick is to store the evidence at the same time you store the score. ### Minimum evidence set (what to store per supplier) Store these fields for every supplier you score, even if you do it in a simple sheet: | Evidence field | Why it matters | Example | | --- | --- | --- | | Supplier page URL | Proves which seller entity you evaluated | Supplier storefront link | | Listing URL(s) used | Shows the context for price tiers and MOQ | The specific product page you priced from | | Capture date | Marketplaces change fast | Date you pulled the data | | Tiered price snapshot | Defends your price score | Tier table values at target quantity | | Verification signals snapshot | Defends your trust score | Verified status, years active, ratings | | Conversation proof | Defends lead time and sample claims | Chat excerpt or quote summary | If you want to do this without manual copy-paste, exporting the underlying supplier intelligence into a CSV is the cleanest workflow. Use a stable key if you have one (supplier URL is usually best), then compare. In Excel, you can use this pattern: ```excel =IF(ISNUMBER(MATCH([@SupplierURL],Sheet2!$A:$A,0)),"Match","New") ``` That answers the common question “How do I compare two sets of data in Excel for matches?” in a way that works on real supplier exports. If you need a richer approach, Power Query is Microsoft’s recommended tool for repeatable data merges; their [Power Query documentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/power-query-documentation-8c1f2a3a-3c8b-4c0d-9f7d-6a0c7b1a2b4d) is the best starting point. ## Frequently Asked Questions **What should a supplier scorecard look like?** A supplier scorecard should have pass-fail gates for non-negotiables (MOQ, compliance, sample feasibility) and a weighted ranking section for comparisons (tiered price at target quantity, lead time, verification, responsiveness). It should also store evidence links and capture dates so the scoring is auditable. **How do you evaluate your suppliers?** Evaluate suppliers by scoring what varies (price tiers, lead time, response quality, marketplace trust signals) and gating what can break the deal (compliance, identity verification, unworkable MOQ). Always validate with samples and written confirmations, not just marketplace badges. **How do I compare two sets of data in Excel for matches?** Use a stable key like supplier URL, then run MATCH or XLOOKUP to flag duplicates across sheets. If you do this often, Power Query merges are more repeatable and less error-prone than formulas. **What are the four types of suppliers?** A practical sourcing breakdown is manufacturer (factory), trading company, distributor/wholesaler, and service supplier (like packaging or logistics). On Alibaba and 1688, “factory vs trading company” is a useful signal, but you still verify capability through samples, documentation, and responsiveness.
